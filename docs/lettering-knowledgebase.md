# Comic Lettering Knowledgebase

Internal reference for LetterMyComic. This document consolidates industry-standard
comic-lettering craft: conventions, terminology, geometry, and workflow. It is the
source of truth for in-app tooltips, help copy, and feature design. All content is
original wording describing standard techniques and terms of art used across the
comics industry.

---

## 1. The letterer's job

- The letterer builds every text element on the page from the writer's script:
  dialogue balloons, captions, sound effects (SFX), titles and credits, and
  in-world text such as signs, headlines, and phone screens.
- The core purpose of lettering is to guide the reader's eye from element to
  element so the story flow is never in question. Lettering is graphic design in
  service of the story; it must complement the art style and the genre.
- Good lettering does its work without drawing attention to itself. Readers rarely
  notice great lettering, but bad lettering breaks immersion even for readers who
  can't say why. Think of it like a bassline in a music mix: fully present, doing
  essential work, never louder than the other instruments.
- Lettering can itself be a storytelling device — e.g. a wall of overlapping tiny
  balloons to convey a character's unraveling mind, or a mid-scene font change to
  signal possession or broadcast.
- "Rules" in lettering are really industry conventions. They vary by publisher and
  editor, and tight panel space can justify bending them. But conventions exist
  because they keep pages readable — break them deliberately, never by accident.
- Letterers are expected to quietly correct scripts that miss the conventions
  below (writers often don't know them; proofreaders will demand the fixes later).

## 2. Reading order and balloon placement

### The prime directive
- Reading order must never be in question. If a reader has to backtrack to figure
  out who spoke first, the lettering has failed. This rule outranks every
  aesthetic preference.
- Western comics read left-to-right, top-to-bottom — inside each panel, not just
  across the page. The first speaker's balloon must sit higher and/or further
  left than the reply. A reply never sits above the line it answers.

### Placement principles
- Read the negative space the artist left and note where the art steers the eye.
  Balloons act as guideposts; the reading path should trace a smooth curve down
  the page (a gentle S is the classic ideal).
- Cover dead space first (skies, walls, floors); never cover faces, gesturing
  hands, or the focal action. When there is genuinely nowhere clean, remember
  the hierarchy: story clarity beats art preservation.
- Editors sometimes provide balloon "placements" — numbered rough markups over
  the art. Treat them as helpful suggestions: follow them when they're solid,
  override them when you can improve readability. They also warn you early where
  space will be tight.
- Never cross balloon tails — even an implied crossing reads as sloppy work. If
  the artist drew speakers in the "wrong" order, treat it as a layout puzzle:
  cascade balloons vertically in speaking order, tuck balloons between speakers,
  stagger them — there is almost always an in-panel solution.
- To show characters talking over each other, overlap the balloons themselves
  (a later balloon partially covering earlier dialogue) rather than crossing
  tails.
- Avoid tangents: a balloon or SFX outline that just barely grazes an inked art
  line creates visual dissonance. Either clearly overlap the line or clearly
  stay off it — never almost-touch. Fixing one tangent can create another, so
  keep checking.
- Don't center-align a row or stack of balloons with mechanical precision; a
  slight stagger looks organic and carries conversational rhythm. Staggering is
  possible even in cramped panels.

### Balloons and panel borders
- **Butted balloons** (also called anchored or top-lined) sit flat against the
  inside of a panel border. They marry the lettering to the art better than any
  other placement and save considerable space. Never trim a butted balloon at
  its widest point — the curve should bow back inward a little before meeting
  the border so the flat reads as intentional, not like a beheaded oval.
- Floating a balloon over a panel border tends to read as a sticker slapped onto
  the page. Legitimate exceptions: genuinely no room in the art, or an inset
  close-up panel where floating a critical line outside the border creates a
  dramatic beat. Usually there is a more elegant in-panel answer.
- Balloons may deliberately *break* a border and spill into the gutter — an
  attractive space-saver — but only if the balloon fill exactly matches the
  gutter color; otherwise the effect fails in print.

### Balloon stacking conventions
When volume of dialogue or art space forces balloons to stack, pick one
convention and hold it for the whole series:
- **Overlapping** — like dealing cards: first balloon down, each later balloon
  laid on top of its predecessor. A common default; especially effective for
  interruptions.
- **Underlapping** — the traditional big-publisher house style: first balloon on
  top of the stack, each later balloon tucked underneath its predecessor.
- **Foreground/background** — the foreground speaker's balloons always sit on
  top, the background speaker's underneath; sells depth when speakers occupy
  different planes.
- Don't stack every balloon in a conversation. Overlap only the replies that
  should feel like they arrive sooner — stacking is a visual representation of
  conversational rhythm.

## 3. Balloon anatomy and terminology

- **Stroke** — the balloon outline.
- **Negative space** — the empty area between the text and the stroke.
- **Tail** — the pointer that identifies the speaker.
- **Join** — the contact point where two merged balloons meet.
- **Connector** — a longer band linking two balloons; implies a longer pause or
  stitches a back-and-forth conversation together.
- **Off-panel tail** — a tail run to the panel border nearest an unseen speaker.
- **Squink** — the small starburst at the end of a tail against a window, door,
  or wall, showing speech coming from behind or inside that object. Keep squinks
  slightly asymmetrical; the imperfection gives them personality.
- Terminology note: professionals say "balloon," not "bubble" — even a thought
  bubble is a "thought balloon."
- Every balloon style, from craggy monster growls to wavy telepathy, is a
  variation on the same skeleton: a white oval, a black stroke, and a tail.
- Professional balloons are not perfect ellipses — they are squared off very
  slightly (subtle is the goal). Keep two masters: a rounder default and a
  slightly squarer variant for text that stacks rectangularly.
- Interior padding rule of thumb: you should be able to fit one uppercase letter
  of the dialogue font anywhere between the text and the stroke, all the way
  around. The same rule applies inside caption boxes.
- Typical balloon stroke weight is around 0.75 pt at print size (a full 1 pt
  usually reads slightly heavy); it should balance the pen-stroke weight of the
  dialogue font and roughly match the art's line weights.

### Tails
- A tail must unambiguously identify the speaker. Aim at the speaker's head —
  never the shoulder or feet. Thought-balloon tails also point at the thinker's
  head.
- The cardinal rule: whatever its direction, length, or curve, a tail must
  appear to emanate from the balloon's **center**. Off-center tails look wrong
  immediately.
- Typical length: about 60–70% of the distance from balloon to speaker; go
  closer only to single out one speaker in a distant group. Length is also
  constrained by available art room and tangent avoidance.
- Base width rule of thumb: the tail should be about as wide as the letter O of
  the dialogue font where it meets the balloon — constant regardless of balloon
  size or tail length. Mixed fat-and-skinny tails on one page is a classic
  amateur tell.
- Tails taper steadily from base to tip. Avoid needle-point tails that thin into
  a hairline — they vanish against inked art.
- Curved tails read as more human; straight tails suit urgency, shouts, or tight
  spaces. Keep the tail style consistent across a page.
- "Reflections don't speak": when a character is visible only in a mirror, point
  the tail (or an off-panel tail) toward the actual person, not the reflection —
  unless the mirror itself is genuinely the speaker.
- Off-panel speakers: run the tail from the balloon toward the panel border
  nearest the unseen speaker, trimmed cleanly at the border.

### Joined balloons and connectors
- Splitting one speech across multiple joined balloons expresses *beats* —
  pauses in the speech. One giant balloon reads as a monotone run-on. Separate
  balloons also split direct addresses to different characters.
- The seam between joined balloons stays **open**: the interiors flow together
  and no outline crosses the junction.
- In a chain of three or more joined balloons, put the speaker tail on the first
  or last balloon of the series, not a middle one.
- A **connector** is a longer arched band between two balloons of the same
  speaker (e.g. balloons 1 and 3 with another speaker's balloon 2 between).
  Standard style: a wider base on the earlier balloon tapering to a slightly
  narrower base on the later one, tracing a smooth continuous arc through the
  centers of the balloons it links. The band's two edges stay relatively
  parallel — it is a band, not a wedge.
- Reading-order aid: a balloon interposed between two connected balloons should
  tuck slightly into the horizontal gap between them.
- An organic alternative join: let the second balloon overlap the first with a
  visible break in the overlapped outline at the junction — the interiors still
  read as continuous.

### Balloon types (visual vocabulary)
- **Speech (standard)** — white fill, black stroke, pointed tail.
- **Thought** — bumpy cloud outline; tail is a chain of shrinking circles arcing
  toward the thinker's head. Out of fashion (largely replaced by internal
  monologue captions) but still a required skill. Slightly sheared, irregular
  bumps look more hand-drawn than uniform ones.
- **Whisper** — two standard treatments: a dashed balloon outline (the classic),
  or text reduced to roughly 75% size inside an oversized balloon with generous
  negative space. Either is fine; pick one per series.
- **Burst/shout** — three main designs: a scalloped burst (irregular points
  joined by wide sweeping arcs — the dominant modern choice), a classic spiky
  starburst (usually needs no separate tail; drag one point outward instead),
  and a double-outline balloon (a second offset ring around a standard balloon).
  A fourth device is dialogue physically erupting out of the balloon. Pick ONE
  shout style per series and use it with restraint — shout balloons scattered
  everywhere cheapen the page.
- **Radio/electronic** — for any voice via speaker or transmitter: phones,
  radios, PA, screens. Classic version: an all-points spiky outline. Modern
  version: a normal ellipse with small spike clusters at the corners. The tail
  is a zigzag lightning bolt. Radio-balloon text is conventionally italic.
  Point spacing and spike size should read uniformly across a page — small
  balloons get fewer points, big ones more, so gaps stay consistent. Never
  stretch one saved burst shape to fit every text block.
- **Weak/wavy** — sick, dying, drunk, woozy, or ghostly speech: a wobbly
  freehand outline, a soft wavy tail, often smaller text, optionally a gentle
  wave warp on the text itself. Keep the wobble subtle.
- **Monster** — rough, craggy outline plus primitive brutish display type for
  savage speech. A hand-drawn jagged outline has more character than a filter.
  A black fill with colored stroke and colored text is a classic horror
  treatment — effective but widely considered overused.
- **Robotic** — inorganic squared shapes: a rounded rectangle (square corners
  read as captions, so rounding differentiates machine speech) or interference
  patterns of double dashed outlines; kinked electronic tails.
- **Telepathy** — the goal is intangibility. Common baselines: a thought-balloon
  body with an electric tail and small "energy beam" tick marks at the corners,
  or a wavy balloon with colored text/stroke and a stretched echo duplicate
  behind it. Color-coding per telepath keeps multi-voice psychic scenes
  readable. If the sender isn't in the panel, drop the tail and let the balloon
  free-float.
- **Organic/imperfect** — a balloon only *slightly* more irregular than a
  perfect ellipse lends a hand-inked feel. Less is more: if the reader notices,
  it's too much. A calligraphic (thick/thin) outline stroke pairs well with
  this and ties lettering to brush- or nib-inked art.

## 4. Text stacking and line breaks

- Perfect the dialogue text for the whole page **before** drawing any balloons.
  The text stack's silhouette is the foundation of the balloon's shape; sloppy
  stacks force a ripple of balloon re-edits later.
- Target silhouettes:
  - Floating balloon: an ovoid stack — short top line, longer middle lines,
    short bottom. Put the longest words mid-stack when possible.
  - Butted balloon: a flat top that flares out then sweeps back in toward the
    bottom.
  - Caption: as rectangular a block as possible, minimizing ragged line ends.
- Bad stacking is the classic amateur giveaway. Avoid: one over-wide middle
  line; a lone tiny word on top with wide lines below; even block-like line
  lengths inside an oval balloon; any stack that creates lopsided negative
  space. Judge by the negative space between text and balloon wall — unbalanced
  space means wrong breaks.
- **Orphans and widows** (comics usage): a lone short word at the top (orphan)
  or bottom (widow) of a balloon stack. Rule of thumb: rebreak if the lone word
  is three letters or fewer; four-plus is acceptable. Leave it when the dialogue
  is too short to fix or when fixing it produces an uglier balloon.
- Style bold/italic emphasis **before** finalizing line breaks — emphasis
  changes line widths and therefore the stack.
- The squeeze cheat: a too-wide line can be horizontally scaled down to no less
  than about 94% width to fix a stack; below that the compression becomes
  visible. Apply per line, never to a whole balloon.
- Hyphenation: hand letterers hyphenated freely; digital lettering mostly
  dropped it and some publishers ban it. Rule of thumb: don't hyphenate if a
  decent ovoid stack is achievable; if the choice is torturing line breaks
  versus hyphenating a word that splits cleanly and unambiguously, hyphenate.
  Automatic hyphenation should always be off — a hyphen is a human decision.
- Many differently proportioned stacks of the same sentence can all be
  acceptable; choose per the room the art gives you.

## 5. Dialogue grammar

### The crossbar I
- The capital I with bars top and bottom (**crossbar I** / barred I) is reserved
  for the personal pronoun and its contractions: I, I'm, I'll, I've, I'd. Every
  other capital I — including inside all-caps words like BIG or IT'S — uses the
  plain bar-less stroke (slash I).
- Optional extensions some letterers allow: acronyms (C.I.A.) and Roman
  numerals (WWII).
- Professional comic fonts map the slash I to lowercase i and the crossbar I to
  uppercase I — which is why you never letter with caps lock on. Some fonts
  auto-correct misuse via contextual alternates.
- The convention originated as a legibility aid under poor printing; today it is
  the single most commonly spotted amateur tell when done wrong.

### Ellipses, double dashes, continuation
- An **ellipsis** (…) means speech trailing off or fading. If a balloon ends
  with an ellipsis and the sentence continues into a joined balloon, the next
  balloon must **open** with an ellipsis to signal the pickup.
- A **double dash** (--) marks an abrupt stop or interruption, and stands in for
  the semicolon (semicolons are avoided in American comic dialogue). The same
  continuation rule applies: end on a double dash, and the attached next balloon
  starts with a double dash.
- Mainstream US convention: no space before or after an ellipsis or double
  dash. US comics traditionally use the double hyphen where UK/European books
  may use an em dash.

### Emphasis
- The standard dialogue emphasis is **bold italic** on the stressed word — from
  a slight vocal push to a shout. Plain bold alone is almost never used in
  dialogue.
- Punctuation following an emphasized or italic word takes the same style.
- Emphasis choices belong to the writer (marked as underline or bold in the
  script); letterers improvise emphasis only with an established writer's
  blessing.
- Use it sparingly: when everything is stressed, nothing is.
- For screams beyond bold italic, treat the word as a mini sound effect: a
  bigger complementary display face, tweaked letterforms, a slight shear —
  seated back into the line, or bursting out of the balloon entirely. Read the
  character's face in the art to judge intensity.

### Italic conventions
Italics carry specific meanings (punctuation after an italic word is italic too):
- Book, movie, and other titles (as in prose).
- Internal-monologue and omniscient-narrator captions (a common differentiator).
- Ship names (sea or space).
- Foreign words not adopted into English.
- Non-word vocalizations (ugh, huh, oof) when not shouted — often wrapped in
  breath marks.
- Transmitted voices: radio, phone, speaker, broadcast.
- Song lyrics (usually decorated with music notes).
- Thought balloons and telepathy (optional but common).
- Reversal rule: when already-italic text (e.g. a broadcast) contains a normally
  italic item (e.g. a ship name), flip that item back to regular.

### Breath marks
- Small triple-dash clusters flanking coughs, sputters, gasps, and other
  non-language utterances — nicknamed cat whiskers, fireflies, or crow's feet.
- The enclosed word is usually italic (sometimes lowercase or bold italic).
  Writers indicate them with > < around a word; in most comic fonts the glyphs
  live on the brace keys ({ opening, } closing).
- An opening and closing mark drawn together as a tiny burst is a convention for
  a character fading into unconsciousness or dying mid-word.

### Grawlix and shouted questions
- **Grawlix** — the string of typographic symbols standing in for profanity;
  works inline in dialogue or as a standalone exploding burst.
- Shouted questions use ?! (the historically preferred big-publisher order),
  !? or ?!? — house style decides; excessive script strings (??!?!?) may be
  truncated by the letterer. The interrobang glyph exists but has never caught
  on in comics.

### Case, numbers, and small details
- Most comic dialogue is set in all-caps faces. The tradition began as
  insurance against bad newsprint (larger counters didn't clog with ink
  spread), and all-caps also gives uniform line-to-line negative space so lines
  can pack tighter. Sentence case appears occasionally for hushed tones or
  stylized books.
- Numbers: small numbers are usually spelled out in dialogue; larger numbers
  may be numerals as a space saver; dates and designations are always numerals;
  Roman numerals stay letters.
- Script cleanup pass before lettering (find and replace): double spaces to
  single; strings of periods to a true ellipsis; em/en dashes to the double
  dash; straight quotes to smart (curly) quotes. Dialogue arrives in sentence
  case, never typed in all caps.
- Foreign-language dialogue shown in translation is enclosed in < angle
  brackets >, usually italic; the first such balloon gets an asterisk pointing
  to an editor's note naming the language.
- Murmuring or deliberately unreadable dialogue: either small scribbled
  m-squiggles that suggest words, or a run of unspaced ellipsis dots in a small
  balloon with extra negative space.
- Common script descriptors and their standard treatments: (OFF) = off-panel
  tail; (WHISPER) = dashed outline or reduced text; (BURST) = shout balloon;
  (WEAK) = wobbly balloon, wavy tail, smaller text; (SINGING) = italic plus
  music notes; (THINKING) = thought balloon.

## 6. Captions

### Types and their signals
Captions are thoughts not spoken, dialogue heard but not seen, or reader-only
information. Different kinds need instant visual differentiation:
- **Spoken / voice-over** — a speaking character who must be "heard" but is
  genuinely absent from the scene (not merely off-camera — that's an off-panel
  tail). Center-justified, regular dialogue weight, with the quote rules below.
  Often tinted with the speaker's signature color.
- **Internal monologue** — private thoughts; the modern replacement for thought
  balloons. No quotation marks; usually italic; often left-justified to
  further distinguish from spoken captions; typically shares the character's
  caption color scheme.
- **Location/time locator** ("The city, years later") — signals scene or time
  changes. Historically boxed; modern practice is often uniquely styled
  free-floating text with no box at all. Style is wide open (typewriter for
  noir, weathered plank for a western, pixel text for cyber scenes) as long as
  it's legible and matches the book — fix the treatment in the series style
  guide.
- **Omniscient narrator** — the all-knowing disembodied voice; less common
  today. Executed like internal monologue (usually italic, boxed); must be
  clearly distinguishable by color or box design from every character's
  captions. Banner/scroll boxes suit fantasy narration.
- **Editorial** — the asterisk-referenced aside ("*see issue #3") in the
  editor's voice; the simplest style: italic dialogue font, plain box or none,
  often reduced to about 75% of the standard point size, placed below the panel
  or at the page bottom. Must not be confusable with any other caption style in
  the series.
- Specialty variants: journal/diary captions (torn-paper or notebook-page
  boxes — effective but tedious to correct, use sparingly) and text-message
  captions (rounded-rectangle screen bubbles, no outline, white text on a
  medium fill, alignment and tail direction alternating by sender).

### Voice-over quote rules
- Every spoken caption **opens** with double quotes.
- A lone spoken caption also closes with quotes.
- In an uninterrupted series by one speaker, only the **last** caption gets
  closing quotes; intermediate captions get none.
- Closing quotes signal "this speaker is finished for now" — so they also
  appear whenever the speaker is interrupted or hands off.
- When a voice-over scene cuts back to the speakers in person, dialogue reverts
  to balloons.
- Smart (curly) quotes, not straight ones.

### Caption mechanics
- Rectangular text blocks; break lines to minimize ragged ends. Caption boxes
  share the balloon spec (thin black outline, optional fill) and the same
  interior-padding rule (about one uppercase O of clearance).
- Color-coding is the easiest way to tie a caption to a character; base the
  palette on the character's costume or clothing. A simplified emblem inset in
  the box is a mainstream trick.
- Design for frequency: before committing to an ornate caption style, consider
  how often it must recur — elaborate, hard-to-resize designs become a burden
  across a long series.
- A barely-perceptible roughening of the box outline (checked at print size)
  gives a hand-inked feel; keep it so subtle the reader never consciously sees
  it.

## 7. Sound effects (SFX)

### Design principles
- The job is translating a sound's qualities — pitch, mass, texture, delicacy —
  into a visual. The script supplies the onomatopoeia; the letterer sells it.
  Trust your first mental image of the sound.
- Considerations: emotional weight (a roar should feel more visceral than a
  squeak); panel space (tight space suggests condensed letterforms, a wide slot
  suggests extended ones); harmony with the art style (painterly art wants
  organic brush SFX; vintage art wants classical forms); and the sound's source
  and perspective.
- **The cardinal sin is "sticker" SFX** — flat type floating above the art with
  no relationship to it. Identify where the sound originates and which
  direction its energy travels, then make the lettering obey: explosions push
  outward from an epicenter, approaching sounds grow toward the reader, sirens
  swell and recede in a wave.
- Boring is worse than bad in display lettering: creative letter positioning,
  slight perspective, a gentle arc, shear, and per-letter scaling all beat a
  straight horizontal word.
- Color: letterers usually work over inks before colors exist, which is why
  yellow SFX are everywhere — yellow reads against almost any palette. When
  colored art exists, sample the palette from it.
- Typing a word in a beefy font is not a design. A typeface is a starting
  point; energy comes from working the letterforms — per-letter scaling,
  shearing, overlapping, tailored anchor-point edits.
- Restraint and consistency: pick treatments per series; a lone one-off style
  in a book of uniform strokes looks out of place.

### Standard techniques
- Work sequence: place and size the SFX over the art first; convert to outlines
  for full letterform freedom (SFX rarely change in proofs); effects come last.
- An outline (offset contour) keeps SFX readable where they cross inked lines.
  Stacked multiple contours in complementary colors read as raw energy — fire,
  electricity — escalating outward (e.g. light core, warm middle, hot rim).
- A duplicated, offset, dark copy behind the letters adds cheap dimensionality.
- Thin-inside/thick-outside contours: outline each letter thinly, then outline
  the outlines; the result reads as fine lines between letters but a heavy
  contour around the whole word.
- **Hollow SFX** — outline-only letters with transparent interiors let a big
  effect sit in a detail-packed panel without hiding the art.
- Hiding SFX behind art: masking an effect partially behind a drawn element
  buys more panel space and sells integration — trace the art's inked edge
  precisely where the SFX crosses it; a sloppy trace kills the illusion.
- Repeated SFX (a ringing phone, a dripping tap) should not be identical
  clones: vary the letterforms slightly per instance and resize/rotate each.
  Escalate size across successive panels to suggest growing intensity.
- Warp presets (arcs, flags, waves) are available but easily overdone — a
  little goes a long way, and hand-arranged letters usually beat a canned
  filter. Dialogue can be designed as an SFX (a monster's roar) when a balloon
  can't deliver the intensity — it's still dialogue.
- Punctuating SFX is contested: purists argue a noise needs no exclamation
  point because the design carries the intensity; others keep the script's
  punctuation. Either way: consistent, and never more than one "!".
- **Roach chew** — term of art for small distressed nicks that give letters a
  grimy, aged look; a hand-lettering-era touch used on titles and SFX.
- Truncating an over-long scripted onomatopoeia while preserving its flavor is
  accepted letterer's license (check with the editor if unsure).

## 8. Page geometry and templates

### Standard US comic page (letter at print size)
- **Full bleed**: 6.875" x 10.4375" — the outer zone that gets trimmed away.
  Nothing meaningful lives here unless it deliberately runs off the page.
- **Trim**: 6.625" x 10.1875" — the physical cut line.
- **Safe area**: 6.125" x 9.6875" — keep all balloons, captions, and SFX inside
  to guarantee they survive printing. Panel borders normally live here.
- Bleed margin between trim and bleed edges: 0.125" per side.
- Original art is traditionally drawn oversized (11" x 17" board with a roughly
  10" x 15" live area) and reduced for print.
- Double-page spreads: 13.5" x 10.4375" at full bleed. There is **no bleed
  between the facing pages** — bleed applies only to the top, bottom, and outer
  edges; guides are book-matched (mirrored) across the spread.
- Webcomics change the math: build the template at the delivery format (e.g. a
  tall vertical-scroll strip), work in RGB, and bump type size and stroke
  weights roughly 150–175% versus print so lettering stays legible on phones.

### Working template conventions
- A lettering template carries: the artboard at full-bleed size, guides for
  bleed/trim/safe, layer structure (bottom to top: art, beneath-balloons,
  balloons, SFX, lettering, plus a production-only title block), and a
  pasteboard library of reusable assets — balloon shapes in many sizes, caption
  styles, tails, connectors.
- The art layer is locked and dimmed (~30%) so lettering reads clearly while
  working; dimming never affects export.
- The title block (title/issue/page/letterer) sits outside the safe area and is
  deleted from final files.
- Keep pristine backups of blank templates — accidentally saving a lettered
  page over the template is inevitable eventually.
- Reusable assets beat redrawing: letterers never draw balloons fresh each time;
  they duplicate masters and reshape. Fiddly shapes (electric tails, thought
  clouds, bursts, squinks) are pre-built in libraries at multiple sizes because
  stretching one saved shape distorts its details.

## 9. Print preparation

### Ink and overprint discipline
- All everyday lettering black is **flat 100% K** (C0 M0 Y0 K100) — never rich
  black — for both fills and strokes.
- Set every K:100 fill and stroke to **overprint** (print on top of the other
  ink plates). This is insurance against misregistration halos where black
  lettering sits on colored art.
- Only black overprints. A colored fill or white text set to overprint is a
  print defect waiting to happen (overprinting white disappears entirely;
  overprinting a color over black muddies it). Audit overprint settings on
  every object as each page is finished, and again before export.

### Resolution and color
- Print work is CMYK; screen work is RGB. Verify colors after any conversion —
  they can shift noticeably.
- High-res print art: 400–600 ppi. Don't letter over art below roughly 120 ppi.
- Never upscale low-resolution art — upscaling adds pixels, not detail. Request
  proper files instead. Downscaling is fine.
- 300 ppi CMYK is the common working-template floor; match final raster export
  resolution to the art's own ppi.

### Deliverables and file hygiene
- Ask before lettering begins whether the client wants lettering-only files
  (their production merges lettering with high-res art) or full print-ready
  pages — the latter is a separately billed production service.
- Lettering-only deliverables: vector files of balloons/captions/SFX with the
  art and title-block layers removed. Text is converted to outlines first.
- Never deliver live editable fonts: shipping font files can violate the font's
  license, and it invites someone else to alter credited work. Convert all text
  to outlines before delivery — and never overwrite the working file with the
  outlined version, or corrections become impossible.
- Ask whether the book will be translated: flattened raster finals are hostile
  to translation (the next letterer must manually paint out every balloon);
  vector lettering or lettering-on-its-own-layer files save enormous effort.
- Flattened raster finals: CMYK, resolution matched to the art, lossless
  compression; open the exported files afterward to spot-check — especially
  overprints on colored SFX, where a mistakenly overprinting bright color
  renders muddy.
- Proofs are low-resolution PDFs of the whole book for editor review; keep the
  proof pipeline one-click repeatable, because correction rounds ("correx") are
  expected on nearly every book.

## 10. Style guides

- On a new series the letterer may propose the entire lettering design:
  typefaces, balloon style, caption treatments, SFX approach, stroke policy.
  Test pages in a few candidate styles help the creative team choose.
- Decision inputs: genre (a western wants weathered signage captions; a police
  procedural wants clean modern shapes and maybe caution-tape motifs), the
  script's density (dialogue-heavy books may justify border-breaking balloons
  to save space; sparse scripts allow airy negative space), the writer's notes,
  and above all the art team — study the inking style, approximate the line
  weights in your strokes, and match the coloring texture.
- A style guide is a deliberate package: balloon shape, caption system, fonts,
  stroke weight and treatment, shout style, stacking convention, whisper
  treatment, SFX palette. Keep it consistent within a series and vary it
  between series, even with the same creative team.
- Plan a treatment for every script descriptor variation (whisper, burst, weak,
  singing, foreign) up front when building the guide.
- One dialogue face for all ordinary human speech; distinct voices (robots,
  monsters, ghosts, broadcasts) earn their own face or styling precisely
  because the baseline is consistent. Restraint: many voice fonts in one book
  becomes noise.
- Consistency mechanics: keep named, pre-styled samples of every balloon and
  caption style in the series template and copy/paste them; record repetitive
  operations as one-key automations so attention stays on creative decisions.

## 11. Typography and fonts

- Comic-specific typefaces are effectively mandatory: they carry the dual I
  logic (crossbar I on uppercase, slash I on lowercase), breath-mark glyphs,
  and true bold-italic emphasis weights. General-market fonts lack these.
- Preferred format is OpenType; quality comic fonts include auto-ligatures and
  contextual alternates that subtly vary repeated letters so type reads more
  hand-lettered — check for these when font shopping.
- Dialogue faces come in two classic pen flavors: calligraphy-nib style (thin
  verticals, thick horizontals, wedge terminals) and tech-pen style (uniform
  round strokes). Match the choice to the art's inking.
- No industry-standard point size exists; dialogue typically falls around
  7.5–9.5 pt at print size, varying by font design. Print-test candidate sizes;
  when unsure, go slightly larger — a bit too big beats unreadable.
- Leading: set sample lines in the boldest weight (bold italic), tighten until
  ascenders and descenders almost collide, then add a hair of room — if bold
  clears, regular and italic will too. Built-in font defaults usually need
  adjusting.
- "Typeface" is the family; "font" is one member (the Italic, the Bold). The
  distinction has blurred in common use.
- Type anatomy vocabulary worth knowing: baseline, cap height, x-height,
  ascender/descender, counter, bowl, crossbar, stem, terminal, spine, kerning
  versus tracking/spacing.
- Font licensing: you buy a license, not the font. Never redistribute font
  files; deliver outlined text instead. Use commercially licensed faces for
  commercial work.

## 12. Workflow and professional benchmarks

### Order of operations (per page)
1. Read the entire script first; pre-clean it (ellipses, dashes, smart quotes).
2. Read the art: negative space, tight spots, reading path, placement roughs.
3. Place all text blocks and paste script lines; apply emphasis styling.
4. Perfect every stack and line break. Text is final before any balloon exists.
5. Lock the text; balloon every block, matching balloon to stack silhouette.
6. Tails, joins, connectors.
7. SFX last (dialogue claims space first).
8. Final pass: hunt tangents and errors, audit overprints, ask whether any
   placement change would improve the read, clean up stray assets, update the
   page label, save.

### Benchmarks
- An experienced letterer works from internalized muscle memory at roughly
  15–20 minutes per page once up to speed — a standard issue in about a day's
  session, with at least half an issue per day a common professional floor.
- Titles and credits are expected of every letterer and are designed inside the
  page rate — keep them quick (tens of minutes, not hours), simple, and
  complementary to the art. Logos are a separate, specialized, higher-priced
  discipline.
- Corrections ("correx") arrive on nearly every book — typically small fixes,
  a pass usually under an hour, two or three passes per issue. Normal correx
  are included in the rate; wholesale rewrites after lettering deserve
  renegotiation. Do your own corrections — your name is in the credits.
- Reliability (on-time delivery, fast correction turnaround, no drama) is what
  keeps letterers hired more than raw flash.
- Track every project's stage (files received, proofs delivered, finals
  delivered, invoiced, paid) in one at-a-glance sheet, and invoice immediately
  on delivering finals.

### Design judgment
- The difference between good and great lettering is graphic design sense:
  hierarchy, negative space, restraint, and harmony with the art.
- The rule of odd numbers: odd counts of visual elements (e.g. three distinct
  units in a title design) tend to please the eye more than even counts.
- Assemble all elements of a display design before judging it; adjust one
  change at a time and reassess the whole.
- Legibility outranks style everywhere: a display treatment that can't be read
  at arm's length needs a redesign, and every design should survive grayscale.
- The squint test: viewed as pure shapes, the lettering on a page should trace
  a clean path through the panels toward the page turn.
